# Extending a Graph Transformer Voter Model: Five Implementable Analytics Frameworks

## In Plain Terms
Before the technical detail, here is what each objective actually does and why it matters — no math required. Each one answers a practical question a campaign has to make decisions about.

**1. Which races need help, and where?** The model already makes an educated guess about every individual voter — how likely they are to vote, and whether they lean our way. This objective adds those guesses up, district by district, to answer three separate questions: Which races are genuine toss-ups? Where do we have supporters who simply don't turn out (votes we're leaving on the table)? And where are there enough truly undecided voters that persuasion could tip the result? A crucial piece is being honest about how *uncertain* the forecast is. Add up a million individual guesses and the arithmetic will claim the district is decided to within a fraction of a percent — which no one should believe. The real uncertainty comes from the chance that the model misreads the whole environment at once, and the framework measures that against what actually happened in 2022. The practical output is a ranked list of races by "need per dollar," since a small, close local seat is far cheaper to move than a giant congressional one.

**2. Where are the pockets of supporters who don't vote?** Voting is socially contagious: knock on one door and you often lift the whole household, and to a lesser degree the block. The best use of limited canvassing hours is therefore to find tight geographic clusters of people who support us but rarely vote — where one visit spills over onto several nearby votes. This objective uses the model to group voters not just by who they resemble (age, party) but by who they are actually connected to, then ranks those clusters by how much support is bottled up in them, how much of it could realistically be unlocked, and how close together the doors are. Three hundred low-turnout supporters packed into a few blocks is a completely different opportunity than the same three hundred scattered across the county.

**3. Who gives money together?** Donors cluster — people tend to give to the same causes and candidates as those around them, and mapping those giving communities helps target fundraising. Two honest limits shape this. First, public donation records only capture a small, unusually wealthy and engaged slice of people (smaller donations are reported with no name attached at all), so "no record of giving" absolutely does not mean someone doesn't give. Second, matching a name and address on a donation filing to the right person in the voter file is genuinely error-prone and has to be done carefully, because bad matches invent relationships that were never there.

**4. Who is actually worth asking for money?** The instinct is to rank people by how likely they are to donate — but that is the wrong list. It puts people who would have given anyway at the top, so you spend money to buy donations you already had. What you actually want is the people who give *because you asked* — the extra dollars your outreach causes that wouldn't have happened otherwise. Measuring that "lift" honestly requires an experiment: ask a random group, hold back a comparable group, and compare. The framework also folds in practical realities — email costs almost nothing while a live phone call costs a few dollars, and someone who has already given the legal maximum can't give more no matter how eager they are.

**5. Who pulls other people along?** Some voters, if they participate, bring others with them — and it's tempting to hunt for these "influencers" across the whole network. The honest version is much narrower. We can genuinely trust that housemates influence each other, because the evidence for household spillover is strong; but "lives in the same precinct" is not a real relationship and shouldn't be treated as influence. So rather than an elaborate and shaky model of who sways whom across the county, the reliable move is simple: score each person by how many likely-supporter-but-unlikely-to-vote housemates they have. That's a lever we can trust, because it's built only on connections we can verify.

**Putting it together.** The real prize isn't five separate ranked lists that someone has to reconcile by hand. It's combining every voter's scores — likelihood to vote, which way they lean, expected donation, which cluster they sit in, how many housemates they'd bring along — into a single plan that spends a fixed budget across mail, phone, email, and door-knocking to win the most votes. That is one coordinated decision instead of five competing to-do lists.

**One honest thread runs through all of it.** The first objective can be checked against reality — we'll know on election night whether the district forecasts were right. But the "who should we contact" objectives (2, 4, and 5) can't be graded that way. The only way to know whether the targeting actually *worked*, rather than merely looked sensible, is to deliberately leave a small, randomly chosen group untouched as a comparison. Building that holdout into the plan from the start is what separates a campaign that learns from one that just spends.

## TL;DR
- All five objectives can be built as thin, cheap layers on the three assets the existing GraphGPS/PyG model already produces (task-head predictions, penultimate node embeddings, raw heterogeneous topology); the highest-leverage additions are (a) a Poisson-binomial + district random-effect aggregator calibrated on 2022 backtest residuals for "need," and (b) a hurdle + uplift stack for donor ROI that separates propensity from incremental treatment effect.
- The hardest and most-often-botched point across objectives 2, 4, and 5 is validation asymmetry: turnout/support validate against precinct returns, but clustering, donor ROI, and influence are *interventions* that only truly validate against a randomized (ideally household/cluster-randomized) holdout — reserve 5–10% of every treated segment as untreated control.
- Influence maximization should be treated with deep skepticism: its output is only as trustworthy as per-edge activation probabilities p_uv, which are defensible only for household edges; prefer the parameter-free "household leverage" score for production and reserve IMM/diffusion models for research.

## Key Findings
- Naive independent aggregation of per-voter Bernoulli predictions to district level produces a Poisson-binomial whose variance Σ p_i(1−p_i) drastically understates real uncertainty because it ignores correlated/systematic error; the fix is a district/cycle random effect calibrated from backtest residuals, not assumed.
- Supervised GNN embeddings only surface structure the training objective (turnout, support) already deemed predictive; a self-supervised head (Deep Graph Infomax, GraphMAE) trained alongside is required to get task-agnostic relational structure for honest clustering.
- Household turnout spillover is real and large — Nickerson 2008 found "60% of the propensity to vote is passed onto the other member of the household" (direct effect ~9.8pp, pass-along ~6pp), and the Gerber-Green-Larimer 2008 "Neighbors" mailing "raised turnout among registered voters by 8.1 percentage points" — which is the entire justification for targeting dense low-turnout clusters and for the household-leverage influence score.
- Itemized FEC/NY-BOE donor data covers only a small, selected slice of voters, so donation edges are missing-not-at-random; entity resolution (Splink/Fellegi-Sunter) with measured false-match rates is a prerequisite, and "absence of a donation edge is not evidence of non-donation."
- Ranking donors by P(donate) optimizes the wrong quantity; incremental ROI is a heterogeneous treatment effect requiring randomized/quasi-random solicitation variation, uplift meta-learners (X-learner) or causal forests, and household/cluster randomization to respect SUTVA.

## Details

### Cross-cutting architecture: the shared encoder and multi-head design
The existing model is a GraphGPS-style graph transformer (Rampášek et al., 2022, "Recipe for a General, Powerful, Scalable Graph Transformer," NeurIPS) in PyTorch Geometric, combining local message passing with a global attention module and positional/structural encodings (Laplacian eigenvectors / random-walk structural encoding). It emits penultimate node embeddings h_i ∈ R^d and task heads. The design principle for all five objectives is to add heads and self-supervised objectives to this shared trunk rather than train separate models, so that turnout, support, donation, and structural signals share representation and are mutually calibrated.

Concretely, the heterogeneous graph is a PyG `HeteroData` object with a `voter` node type and multiple edge types keyed by relation triples, e.g. `('voter','household','voter')`, `('voter','same_address','voter')`, `('voter','precinct','voter')`, `('voter','geo_knn','voter')`, and — after objective 3 — `('voter','donates_to','committee')` / `('committee','rev_donates_to','voter')` and `('voter','coworker','voter')`. A homogeneous GNN module is lifted to the heterogeneous graph with `torch_geometric.nn.to_hetero(model, metadata, aggr='sum')`, which duplicates message-passing parameters per relation and aggregates across relations. Task heads are small MLPs on the shared embedding.

### OBJECTIVE 1 — District and candidate "need"

**Literature.** The core statistical object is the Poisson-binomial distribution (PBD): the sum Y = Σ ζ_i of independent non-identical Bernoullis ζ_i ~ Bernoulli(p_i) has E[Y] = Σ p_i and Var(Y) = Σ p_i(1−p_i) (Wang 1993). For large districts a Lyapunov CLT gives Y ≈ Normal(Σ p_i, Σ p_i(1−p_i)); the exact PMF is available by the Fourier/DFT method of Hong (2013, "On computing the distribution function for the Poisson binomial distribution," *Computational Statistics & Data Analysis*), implemented in the R `poisbinom` package and Python ports. The key methodological insight — well documented in the election-forecasting literature (Gelman/Heidemanns Economist model in *Harvard Data Science Review*; Linzer 2013; Lock & Gelman 2010) — is that the PBD variance is an *under*-estimate of real aggregate uncertainty because it assumes independence and, crucially, no systematic error. As the 538/Economist debates showed (Nate Silver: correlated errors are "where often most of the work is in modeling"), between-unit correlation dominates the aggregate variance. The remedy is a hierarchical model with a shared latent shock per district/cycle: logit(p_i) = f(x_i, G) + α_d, with α_d ~ Normal(0, τ²) calibrated from backtest residuals.

**Framework.** Two-stage. Stage 1: the GTN produces per-voter p_i^turnout and p_i^support. Expected two-party vote for the campaign in district d is D_d = Σ_{i∈d} p_i^turnout · p_i^support; expected opposition is Σ p_i^turnout·(1−p_i^support). Stage 2: wrap in a PyMC hierarchical model to inject the correlated shock. For each district, draw α_d ~ Normal(0, τ²) and α_cycle ~ Normal(0, τ_c²), set logit(p_i') = logit(p_i) + α_d + α_cycle, and Monte-Carlo the Poisson-binomial (direct DFT or Gaussian approximation) to get the posterior predictive distribution of net margin. τ is *not* assumed: it is calibrated so that backtested 2022 precinct-level aggregate residuals (predicted minus actual counts) have coverage matching nominal — i.e., choose τ so that ~50% of actual precinct results fall in the 50% predictive interval, ~90% in the 90% interval. This is a calibration/coverage exercise on the empirical distribution of precinct residuals, exactly the "examine the distribution of precinct-level aggregate residuals" the objective calls for.

Operational definitions of "need" as posterior functionals:
- **Competitiveness** = P(0.48 < district vote share < 0.52) or P(flip): the posterior probability the margin crosses zero. Highest where the predictive distribution straddles 50/50.
- **Mobilization gap** = Σ_{i∈d} w(p_i^turnout) · p_i^support, with w() a triangular/beta weight peaking in the movable 0.3–0.7 turnout middle and ≈0 for near-zero-propensity non-voters and near-certain voters. This targets persuadable-to-mobilize supporters, not unreachable non-voters.
- **Persuasion pool** = Σ_{i∈d} p_i^turnout · H(p_i^support) where H is binary entropy −p log p −(1−p)log(1−p), maximal at p=0.5: high-turnout, genuinely-undecided voters.
- **Per-dollar normalization**: divide marginal seats/votes movable by cost, which scales with electorate size — "cost to move a seat" ≈ (votes needed to flip)/(persuadable+mobilizable pool) × cost-per-contact. Rank districts by need-per-dollar, not raw need. (For calibration, the Gerber-Green-Larimer social-pressure mailing cost roughly US$1.93 per additional vote — a useful lower-bound benchmark for mail GOTV cost efficiency.)

**New York geometry.** Nassau/Suffolk legislative, assembly, senate, and congressional districts do not cleanly nest. Model voter→district as a many-to-many sparse membership matrix M (voters × districts, one per office type), and aggregate separately per office: D_office = Mᵀ_office · (p^turnout ⊙ p^support). Never assume a precinct sits in a single assembly district. **Fusion voting**: "candidate" ≠ "party." In NY's disaggregated fusion system the same candidate appears on multiple lines (e.g., a Democrat also on the Working Families line; a Republican also on the Conservative line) and each line's votes are tallied separately before being summed for the candidate. The support head predicts P(supports candidate C); ballot-line choice is a *separate* multinomial slice. Model line choice conditional on candidate support as a small softmax head, because line totals matter for minor-party ballot access — New York's 2020 reform requires a party to field a candidate for governor or president who wins "at least 2% of all votes cast for the office, or 130,000 votes, whichever is greater," re-qualifying every two years (up from 50,000 votes every four years), with statewide petition signatures raised from 15,000 to 45,000. Line totals are thus a meaningful strategic slice even when they don't change who wins.

**Validation.** Backtest on 2022: aggregate predictions to precinct, compare to certified returns, check both point accuracy (MAE of margin) and *calibration of uncertainty* (interval coverage). This is the one objective with a true external ground-truth anchor.

**Failure modes.** (1) Independence assumption → understated variance; mitigated by the α_d random effect. (2) Miscalibrated individual p_i propagates; run isotonic/Platt recalibration of heads before aggregation. (3) Non-nesting geography silently double-counts; enforce the membership matrix. (4) Turnout×support correlation within voter (a supporter may also be higher-turnout) means the product's variance needs the joint, not marginal — sample the joint from the model, don't multiply point estimates.

### OBJECTIVE 2 — Clustering low-turnout voters using graph embeddings

**Literature.** Two families: (a) clustering on learned node embeddings vs tabular features, and (b) direct topological community detection. Embeddings from a GNN capture relational/homophily structure that tabular demographics miss — who is *connected to whom*, not just who *resembles whom*. The circularity concern is central: embeddings from a *supervised* turnout/support model, by construction, only encode structure predictive of those labels. To get task-agnostic structure, add a self-supervised objective: Deep Graph Infomax (Veličković et al., ICLR 2019, arXiv:1809.10341), which maximizes mutual information between patch (node) representations and a global graph summary via a corruption (node-feature permutation) and a discriminator; or GraphMAE (Hou et al., KDD 2022), a masked-feature graph autoencoder. Both can be trained as an auxiliary loss on the shared trunk: L = L_turnout + L_support + λ·L_DGI. For clustering, HDBSCAN (Campello, Moulavi & Sander 2013; McInnes et al. 2017) is the density-based method of choice, but it suffers the curse of dimensionality, so reduce first — PCA to ~50 dims (retaining most variance), optionally UMAP to ~10–15 dims with `min_dist=0` for compact clusters, then HDBSCAN with `min_cluster_size` tuned to canvass-turf scale. As an interpretable topology-only alternative, the Leiden algorithm (Traag, Waltman & van Eck 2019, "From Louvain to Leiden," *Scientific Reports* 9:5233) fixes Louvain's disconnected-community defect and guarantees well-connected communities — run it directly on the household+coworker subgraph (the defensibly social edges), which is more interpretable than clustering embeddings. Use the `leidenalg`/`igraph` Python bindings.

**Social-contagion rationale.** Targeting *dense low-turnout clusters* is justified because turnout is socially contagious. Nickerson 2008 (APSR 102(1):49-57), a placebo-controlled door-to-door canvassing experiment in Denver and Minneapolis before the Sept. 10, 2002 congressional primaries in two-registered-voter households, found the direct effect on the door-answerer was ~9.8pp and the pass-along to the untreated housemate ~6pp — i.e., "60% of the propensity to vote is passed onto the other member of the household." Gerber, Green & Larimer 2008 (APSR 102(1):33-48), a 180,002-household field experiment before Michigan's August 2006 primary, found the social-pressure "Neighbors" mailing "raised turnout among registered voters by 8.1 percentage points" (the own-record "Self" mailing raised turnout 4.9pp). Bond et al. 2012 (*Nature* 489(7415):295-298), the 61-million-person Facebook experiment (60,055,176 users shown the social message during the 2010 US congressional elections), found "the Facebook social message increased turnout directly by about 60,000 voters and indirectly through social contagion by another 280,000 voters, for a total of 340,000 additional votes," with close friends roughly 4× more influential than weak ties. Dense clusters multiply per-door ROI.

**Framework.** Compute embeddings from the trunk (with the DGI auxiliary head). Reduce with PCA→50→UMAP→~12. Cluster with HDBSCAN. For each cluster c define an operational value/ranking:
Value(c) = size_c × turnout_gap_c × support_share_c × spillover_multiplier_c × geo_density_c,
where turnout_gap_c = (achievable turnout − current mean p^turnout), support_share_c = mean p^support, spillover_multiplier_c = 1 + β·(mean household degree) with β ≈ 0.6 from the Nickerson pass-along, and geo_density_c = doors reachable per canvass turf (target ~100–200 doors/turf). This upweights compact, high-support, low-turnout, high-household-connectivity clusters where canvassers can walk efficiently and each contact spills over.

**Validation.** Clusters are an *intervention target*, so validate against a randomized GOTV holdout, not internal metrics alone. Use internal metrics (DBCV for HDBSCAN, modularity for Leiden) for stability, but the real test is a cluster-randomized GOTV experiment (see cross-cutting) measuring whether treating dense clusters yields higher turnout-per-dollar than treating matched dispersed voters.

**Failure modes.** (1) Supervised-embedding circularity → DGI/GraphMAE auxiliary loss. (2) HDBSCAN labeling most voters "noise" → tune min_cluster_size/min_samples, accept noise as "unclusterable, target individually." (3) Precinct/kNN edges dominate embeddings with pure geography → either drop them from the SSL view or cluster on the household+coworker subgraph. (4) Cluster instability across retrains → fix random seeds, report Leiden as the reproducible fallback.

### OBJECTIVE 3 — Donation affinity groups / donation network structure

**Literature & coverage bias.** FEC itemization is required only for contributors whose aggregate exceeds $200 per cycle (FEC.gov "Contributions by individuals file description"); below that, contributions are "unitemized" with no attached name. Only a small single-digit share of registered voters appear as itemized donors, skewed older, higher-income, and more ideological. This is missing-not-at-random: absence of a donation edge is not evidence of non-donation. NY BOE state-level data has its own thresholds and weaker data quality. Any donation-graph feature must be treated as a positive-only, selection-biased signal.

**Graph construction to avoid clique explosion.** Naive "co-contributors to the same committee" edges create huge cliques (every DCCC donor connected to every other). Three fixes: (a) Model donations as a **bipartite heterogeneous graph** with `committee` as its own node type; donation similarity becomes a learned 2-hop path (voter→committee→voter) rather than an explicit clique — implement in PyG `HeteroData` with `('voter','donates_to','committee')` edges and let `to_hetero` learn the message passing (mirroring the PyG heterogeneous link-prediction examples using `SAGEConv` + `to_hetero`). (b) **Size-gate** co-contribution edges: co-giving to a small local committee is signal; co-giving to a national behemoth is noise — weight edges by inverse committee size (TF-IDF-like: rare committees inform more). (c) **Employer edges** from FEC occupation/employer free-text are among the few genuine social ties — add `('voter','coworker','voter')` after normalizing employer strings.

**Entity resolution.** Matching FEC name+address to voter-file records is probabilistic record linkage (Fellegi & Sunter 1969). Use **Splink** (UK Ministry of Justice; Linacre et al. 2022, *IJPDS*), which implements the Fellegi-Sunter model with EM parameter estimation and scales to millions of records across SQL backends (documented as "linking a million records on a laptop in around a minute"). Pipeline: USPS-style address normalization; blocking on e.g. ZIP + surname (or first-initial+surname+ZIP3) to make comparison tractable; comparison functions (Jaro-Winkler on names, exact/level on ZIP); estimate m/u probabilities via EM; threshold the match weight (log₂ Bayes-factor waterfall). Measure false-match rate on a hand-labeled sample; report precision/recall. fastLink (Enamorado, Fifield & Imai 2019) is the R equivalent, and its paper documents the underlying math Splink follows.

**Framework.** Add the committee node type and donation/coworker edges to the HeteroData; retrain (or fine-tune) the trunk with `to_hetero`. Donation-affinity "groups" are then either (a) communities on the size-gated voter-committee bipartite projection (Leiden) or (b) clusters of the enriched embeddings. These feed objective 4.

**Validation.** Entity resolution: labeled-sample precision/recall + false-match rate. Affinity groups: face-validity against known committees + predictive lift for the donation head (objective 4).

**Failure modes.** (1) Clique explosion → bipartite + size-gating. (2) False matches inject phantom donors → tune Splink threshold, prefer precision. (3) Selection bias interpreted as behavior → never model "did not donate" as a negative; treat as unlabeled. (4) Employer free-text noise ("self", "retired", "N/A") → normalize and drop junk tokens before building coworker edges.

### OBJECTIVE 4 — Donor ROI ranking (fundraising outreach prioritization)

**Literature.** Donation amount is zero-inflated and heavy-tailed → a **hurdle model** (Cragg 1971): P(donate) × E[amount | donate], with the positive part on log-amount (Bernoulli-lognormal hurdle). Implement as two additional heads on the shared encoder: a Bernoulli/BCE donate-probability head over all voters, and a Gaussian-NLL/MSE log-amount head masked to observed donors only. The **critical distinction** is propensity vs incremental ROI: ranking by raw P(donate) surfaces people who would give unprompted. True "bang for buck" is the incremental treatment effect τ_i = E[amount | solicited] − E[amount | not solicited], which is *unidentifiable* without variation in who was solicited. This is heterogeneous-treatment-effect / uplift modeling: two-model/T-learner, S-learner, and the **X-learner** (Künzel, Sekhon, Bickel & Yu 2019, *PNAS* 116(10):4156), which is robust under treatment-group imbalance (relevant since solicitation is not 50/50); and **causal forests / generalized random forests** (Wager & Athey 2018 *JASA*; Athey, Tibshirani & Wager 2019). Libraries: EconML (Microsoft) and CausalML (Uber) for meta-learners; the `grf` R package or `econml.grf` for causal forests. These require a randomized or quasi-random solicitation holdout in historical fundraising records — without it, τ_i is not estimable and you are back to propensity.

**SUTVA/interference.** Soliciting one household member affects another, violating SUTVA. Fix by randomizing at the **household or cluster level** (cluster-randomized experiments; Hudgens & Halloran 2008 partial interference; Ugander et al. 2013 graph cluster randomization; Saint-Jacques et al. 2019 ego-cluster designs) — directly connecting to objective 2's clustering, which supplies the clusters.

**Operational refinements.** (1) **Channel assignment** as constrained optimization given per-channel costs (email ~fractions of a cent; direct mail ~$0.50–$1.00/piece; live phone ~$1–$3/completion; SMS constrained by TCPA consent). (2) **Contribution-limit headroom**: per-candidate-per-election limits are indexed by cycle; subtract cycle-to-date giving to get incremental capacity — a maxed-out donor has zero headroom regardless of propensity. (3) **Temporal validation discipline**: train on donations through a cutoff date and predict after it; never train on full-cycle totals (leakage).

**Framework.** Heads: donate-prob (BCE), log-amount (masked Gaussian-NLL). Expected raw value = P(donate)·exp(μ_logamt + σ²/2). Uplift: fit X-learner/causal forest on the trunk embeddings + tabular features using the randomized-solicitation subset; rank by min(τ_i, headroom_i)/channel_cost. Solve channel assignment as a knapsack/assignment problem under budget.

**Validation.** Uplift models validate only against the randomized holdout via Qini/uplift curves (not AUC). Temporal out-of-time split for the hurdle heads.

**Failure modes.** (1) Propensity-as-ROI confusion → uplift on randomized data. (2) No solicitation variation → τ unidentified; instrument or run a deliberate randomized solicitation experiment. (3) SUTVA violation → household/cluster randomization. (4) Leakage from full-cycle totals → temporal cutoff. (5) Heavy tail → log-transform + robust loss; consider a Pareto/log-normal tail check.

### OBJECTIVE 5 — Influencer identification / network influence

**Literature — the definitions disagree.** (a) *Centrality*: degree centrality is nearly useless here because it is dominated by household/precinct size (a big-household or big-precinct voter is "central" for trivial reasons) — compute per edge type or not at all. PageRank is better; **betweenness centrality** (Freeman 1977; Brandes 2001, O(nm) algorithm) is the most useful for finding *bridges* between otherwise-separate communities, but exact computation is infeasible at ~1M nodes (Brandes is O(|V||E|)), so use sampling approximation (Riondato & Kornaropoulos 2016 with VC-dimension ε-guarantees; Geisberger-Sanders-Schultes estimate) via the **NetworKit** library, which implements exact `Betweenness` (Brandes) plus `ApproxBetweenness` (guaranteed ±ε with probability 1−δ) and `EstimateBetweenness` (faster, no guarantee). (b) *Influence maximization*: specify a diffusion model — Independent Cascade with per-edge activation probability p_uv, or Linear Threshold — and use the Kempe, Kleinberg & Tardos (2003, KDD) result that expected spread σ(S) is monotone submodular, so greedy hill-climbing gives a (1−1/e)≈0.63 approximation. Scalable algorithms: CELF/CELF++ (Leskovec et al. 2007 "Cost-effective Outbreak Detection"; Goyal et al. 2011) lazy-greedy exploiting submodularity; and the Reverse Influence Sampling family — RIS (Borgs et al. 2014), TIM/TIM+ (Tang, Xiao & Shi 2014), and **IMM** (Tang, Shi & Xiao 2015, "Influence Maximization in Near-Linear Time: A Martingale Approach," SIGMOD), the right near-linear-time tool with guarantees at 1M-voter scale (implementations: Ripples; various open-source IMM).

**The central caveat.** IM results are only as good as p_uv, and the common literature default (uniform p=0.05, or weighted-cascade 1/in-degree) is arbitrary and indefensible here. p_uv is only calibrable for **household** edges (from the ~0.6 Nickerson pass-along / Gerber-Green-Larimer effect), plausible-but-uncalibrated for **coworker** edges, and effectively **near-zero** for precinct/ED and geographic-kNN co-location edges (these are not social ties). Set edge-type-specific p_uv from evidence where it exists (household ≈ calibrated spillover) and near-zero elsewhere; do not let co-location edges drive the diffusion. Report IM output as scenario analysis, not ground truth.

**Less-speculative alternative — household leverage.** Most of the value without a diffusion model or free parameters comes from a direct score computed entirely on trusted household edges:
leverage_i = Σ_{j∈household(i)} spillover × (1 − p_j^turnout) × p_j^support,
i.e., voter i's value as a mobilization lever = the expected additional supporting votes among their housemates who are likely supporters but unlikely to vote, scaled by the calibrated spillover (~0.6). This is interpretable, parameter-light, uses only defensible edges, and directly captures "who, if mobilized, drags along the most latent supporting votes." Recommend this for production; reserve IMM for research/scenario work.

**Framework.** Build the household(+coworker) subgraph in NetworKit; compute approximate betweenness (bridges) and PageRank as diagnostic features. Compute leverage_i from the household head outputs. For research, run IMM on the household subgraph with calibrated p_uv and near-zero co-location p.

**Validation.** Influence rankings are interventions → validate against a randomized seeding experiment (seed high-leverage vs random, measure downstream household turnout). Betweenness/leverage have no external anchor otherwise.

**Failure modes.** (1) Degree centrality artifacts → per-edge-type or drop. (2) Arbitrary p_uv → edge-type-specific calibration, near-zero for co-location. (3) IM overfitting to graph artifacts (precinct cliques) → restrict to social edges. (4) Betweenness intractability → NetworKit sampling with ε-guarantee.

### Cross-cutting: validation asymmetry and synthesis

**Validation asymmetry.** Turnout and support validate against precinct returns (external anchor). Objectives 2, 4, 5 are interventions with no external anchor — they only truly validate against a randomized holdout. Reserve 5–10% of every treated segment as untreated control, and given network interference use cluster-randomized designs (graph cluster randomization, Ugander et al. 2013; ego-cluster designs, Saint-Jacques et al. 2019) or household-level randomization so spillover doesn't contaminate control. Note the partial-interference tradeoff: cluster randomization controls bias from inter-unit spillover but sacrifices power and can leave residual bias proportional to the number of inter-cluster edges (Eckles et al. 2017), so cluster boundaries should follow the household/coworker communities from objective 2.

**Synthesis — one optimization, not five rankings.** Combine head outputs into a per-voter vector v_i = [p^turnout, p^support, E[donation], cluster_id, leverage_i, τ_i]. The campaign's real problem is constrained budget allocation across channels and voters to maximize expected net votes (and net funds) subject to budget and legal constraints. Formulate as constrained optimization: maximize Σ_i Σ_c x_{ic}·(expected net-vote or net-dollar gain of contacting voter i via channel c) subject to Σ x_{ic}·cost_c ≤ Budget, Σ_c x_{ic} ≤ 1, plus TCPA/headroom constraints. Because GOTV/persuasion value with spillover exhibits diminishing returns as more of a household/cluster is treated, the objective is submodular, so this is submodular maximization under a knapsack/budget constraint — solvable with a (1−1/e)-guarantee greedy (Nemhauser-Wolsey-Fisher 1978; Sviridenko 2004 for the knapsack variant; lazy-greedy/CELF for speed, Mirzasoleiman et al. "Lazier than Lazy Greedy" for stochastic scaling). This reconciles the five signals into a single decision rather than five un-reconciled lists.

## Recommendations
1. **Build objective 1 first** (highest certainty, external validation). Ship the Poisson-binomial + PyMC district-random-effect aggregator; calibrate τ on 2022 precinct residuals to nominal coverage before trusting any interval. Benchmark: 90% predictive intervals should cover ~90% of held-out 2022 precinct margins; if coverage <80%, increase τ. Change trigger: if point MAE is acceptable but coverage fails, the problem is correlated error (raise τ), not the point model.
2. **Add the self-supervised head (DGI) before doing any clustering** (objective 2). Without it, clusters are circular. Benchmark: clusters should predict turnout residuals not explained by demographics; if Leiden-on-households and embedding-HDBSCAN disagree wildly, trust Leiden as the reproducible, interpretable option.
3. **Do entity resolution (Splink) before donation modeling** (objective 3→4). Benchmark: false-match rate <1–2% on a labeled sample; if higher, raise the match-weight threshold and prefer precision over recall.
4. **For donor ROI, do not deploy a propensity ranking as "ROI."** Stand up a randomized (household-level) solicitation holdout this cycle so uplift is estimable next cycle; until then, label propensity outputs honestly as propensity. Benchmark: a positive, monotone Qini curve on the randomized holdout is the threshold to switch from propensity to uplift ranking.
5. **For influence, deploy household-leverage in production and treat IMM as research only.** Escalation trigger to full IM: a randomized seeding experiment showing leverage-based seeding beats random by a margin exceeding experimental noise, AND defensible non-household p_uv estimates from that experiment.
6. **Unify into the budget-allocation optimizer** once ≥3 heads are calibrated; run greedy submodular maximization under the real budget and TCPA/headroom constraints. Re-solve weekly as predictions and cycle-to-date headroom update.

## Caveats
- Effect sizes borrowed from the GOTV literature (Nickerson's ~60% household pass-along and ~9.8pp/~6pp direct/spillover; Gerber-Green-Larimer's +8.1pp Neighbors and +4.9pp Self, at ~$1.93/vote; Bond et al.'s 340,000 total votes with close friends ~4× weak ties) are from other populations, treatments, and eras; treat them as priors to be re-estimated on Nassau/Suffolk experimental data, not constants. The Gerber-Green-Larimer Hawthorne (~2.5pp) and Civic Duty (~1.8pp) figures come from reputable secondary summaries rather than a directly quoted line in the article, so weight them slightly lower.
- All intervention rankings (objectives 2, 4, 5) are unfalsifiable without randomized holdouts; the single biggest risk is shipping a plausible ranking that has never been tested causally.
- FEC/NY-BOE donor coverage is a small, biased slice (itemized only above the $200 aggregate threshold); donation-derived features describe donors, not the electorate, and "no donation edge" is not "did not donate."
- Influence-maximization outputs are highly sensitive to p_uv and should never be presented as precise; the honest deliverable there is household-leverage plus scenario ranges.
- Fusion voting and non-nesting districts are easy to get silently wrong; enforce many-to-many district membership and candidate≠party separation in code, with unit tests against known 2022 ballots (e.g., verify a cross-endorsed candidate's Democratic and Working Families line totals sum correctly and that a precinct maps to the right assembly, senate, and congressional districts independently).
